## 3. Stage 1 — Vehicle Maintenance Logbook

### 3.1 What it is and why it leads

This is the only product you can sell today, so it does the early heavy lifting. It also has a structural advantage the Pip books don't: **vehicle maintenance is a searched, practical, low-emotion purchase.** People look up "how often to change oil" and "car service record book" with a task in mind, which is exactly the intent Pinterest and search reward. Picture books are discovered; logbooks are looked for.

### 3.2 Audience — stated plainly

- **Vehicle owners** keeping their own service records — the core use case for the book.
- **DIY maintenance enthusiasts** — people who change their own oil, track their own intervals, and enjoy a tidy system.
- **Practical gift buyers** — someone buying for a new driver, a first car, a car-obsessed relative, or a small fleet owner.

These are **not** parents of 3–6-year-olds. There is essentially no overlap in Pinterest interests, search phrasing or buying moment, and the copy must not pretend otherwise.

### 3.3 The research gap — named, not papered over

**No research pass has been done on this audience.** Everything below in §3.5 is a starter hypothesis built from how vehicle-maintenance products are generally described, not from observation of your actual buyers. I am not going to invent a persona for it.

What I would research, in this order, all at zero cost:

1. **Pinterest search autocomplete** — type "car maintenance", "vehicle log", "oil change" and record what the platform suggests. That is free, live demand data and it outranks my guesses.
2. **Amazon category bestsellers and their review language** — what the top logbooks promise, what reviewers complain is missing (size, layout, page count, binding). That tells us the pin hooks and the listing gaps.
3. **Competitor titles and subtitles** — the phrase a competitor ranks on is a phrase buyers use.
4. **Gift-intent phrasing** — whether "gift for new driver" or "gift for car lover" behaves as a browse or a search.
5. **What actually gets saved** — after two weeks of pins, Pinterest's own analytics replaces all of the above.

Until 1–4 are done, treat §3.5 as a working list, not a keyword strategy.

### 3.4 Pinterest boards — Stage 1 (five, vehicle-only)

1. Vehicle maintenance log books
2. Car care checklists & service records
3. DIY car maintenance for beginners
4. Gifts for new drivers & first cars
5. Road trip & vehicle prep

Board descriptions carry the phrases in §3.5. **No Pip keyword, no children's-book phrasing and no bedtime language appears on any of these boards.**

### 3.5 Keyword hypothesis — Stage 1 (unmeasured, see §3.3)

Maintenance and record intent:
`vehicle maintenance log book` · `car maintenance tracker` · `car service record book` · `oil change log` · `car maintenance checklist` · `vehicle service log`

DIY intent:
`DIY car maintenance for beginners` · `how often to change your oil` · `car care tips for new drivers`

Gift intent:
`gift for new driver` · `gift for new car owner` · `gift for car lover` · `practical gifts for drivers`

**Rule:** these phrases appear in Stage 1 pin titles, descriptions, board names and alt text — and nowhere in Stage 2 content. The Pip set in §4.5 does not appear here.

### 3.6 Content — what Stage 1 publishes

- **Checklist pins** — "the 8 things to check before a road trip", "what to log at every oil change". The checklist is the value; the logbook is what makes it tidy.
- **Before/after pins** — a messy glovebox receipt pile versus a filled-in logbook page. This is the single most saveable format for this audience.
- **Gift pins** — "a practical gift for a new driver that isn't another air freshener".
- **Capped TikTok (Stage 1 only)** — 3 posts a week, one fixed hour, four weeks: page flips, "what I log and why", a glovebox clear-out. If it sends no clicks to the listing by Day 30, it stops.
- **Facebook DIY and vehicle groups** — participate as a person for two weeks before any mention of the book, and only where rules allow it.

**Images: approved assets only.** The logbook's own cover, real photographs of the real book, or plain type on a plain background. No generated artwork, no stock photography, no AI illustration, no mocked-up cover. If a pin needs a picture we don't have, the pin doesn't get made.

## 5. Where one brand account helps, and where it hurts

You have one brand, and I am not going to recommend running two businesses. But the honest answer is mixed, and this is the part most plans skip.

**Where a single account helps**

- One login, one free scheduler, one set of analytics, one set of boards to maintain. At 5–6 hours a week, that matters more than theoretical relevance.
- The brand name "Cozy Creations by Autumn" is neutral enough to hold both a practical logbook and a children's book without either feeling out of place.
- There is a real, small credibility transfer: a publisher who makes something useful *and* something warm reads as a person, not a keyword farm. Grandparents in particular buy from a face.
- One follow is worth more across two products than two half-followed accounts.

**Where a single account hurts**

- **Pinterest rewards topical focus.** A profile split between vehicle maintenance and bedtime stories gives the algorithm two weaker signals instead of one strong one. Expect slightly slower distribution on both, especially in month one.
- **The follower bases barely overlap.** A person pinning oil-change checklists is not pinning bedtime books. Each stage's pins will underperform with the other stage's followers.
- **The bio can only say one thing well.** A profile bio has room for one promise. Whichever audience it names, the other one arrives to a slightly confusing page.
- **The boards do the work instead of the profile.** Board names, board descriptions, pin titles and alt text carry the keywords. That is a fix, not a cure.

**What I'm doing about it**

- **Keyword targeting moves off the profile and onto the boards.** Profile bio stays short and neutral; each board is keyworded for its own audience.
- **Boards are never mixed.** A Stage 1 pin never lands on a Stage 2 board, and vice versa.
- **Pin titles and descriptions never mix keyword sets.** One pin, one audience, one intent.
- **If the logbook grows faster than Pip**, the honest move in month two or three is a second Pinterest account for the logbook. I'm not recommending it now — two accounts at 5–6 hours a week is how a campaign dies. Revisit it with data, not enthusiasm.

## 6. How the 30 days are shaped

The channel research and the shape of the week are unchanged. What's new is that each week's work is split between the two stages.

### 6.1 The spine: Pinterest, roughly 50–60% of your hours

Vertical pins at 2:3 (1000×1500 px), with the search phrase *on the image*, because that is what Pinterest indexes. Keywords in the pin title, the description, the board name and the alt text. No hashtags — they do nothing there. 3–5 fresh pins a week per stage where you can, scheduled 2–4 weeks ahead using Pinterest's own free scheduler.

**Pin supply across 30 days: roughly 12–16 Stage 1 pins and 8–12 Stage 2 pins.** Stage 1 gets more because it has a destination that can convert today.

### 6.2 The satellites, capped on purpose

- **Facebook** — two different jobs, two different sets of groups. Stage 1: DIY, car-care and vehicle-owner groups. Stage 2: parent, grandparent and kids-book groups. Join 5–10 in total, take part as a person for the first two weeks before mentioning anything, photo + short story beats a link post, no hashtags, never copy-paste the same post across groups.
- **Instagram** — Stage 2 only, credibility rather than discovery. Reels are the only non-follower reach. 2 Reels + 1 carousel a week, 3–5 specific hashtags at most, captions written as searchable sentences. Collab posts with other small kidlit accounts are the free multiplier worth chasing.
- **TikTok** — Stage 1 only, one fixed hour a week, three posts, four weeks, then a decision. Uncapped, it eats the whole project.

### 6.3 Deliberately cut

30-hashtag sets · the same asset cross-posted everywhere · posting to a near-empty Facebook Page · follow-for-follow · engagement pods · bought followers · follow-to-enter giveaways (against platform rules, and they attract prize-hunters) · trend-audio chasing · mass "collab" blasts · incentivised or fabricated reviews · **any generated or substituted artwork**.

### 6.4 The floor version — one per stage

If a week collapses, do only this and nothing breaks:

- **Stage 1 floor:** 3 logbook pins scheduled, one genuine group comment.
- **Stage 2 floor:** 1 typographic Pip pin scheduled.

That keeps both compounding assets alive. Skipping a week entirely is the only real failure mode in month one.

## 11. What this plan cannot do

- **It cannot make sales happen.** Organic reach to a cold audience with no reviews is slow. Anyone who promises you sales in 30 days at $0 is guessing or selling something.
- **It cannot measure what happens after the click.** Pinterest tells us someone left for the listing. It cannot tell us they bought. Only your KDP reports can.
- **It cannot sell a book that isn't published**, and Stage 2 does not try. There is no Stage 2 sales number because there is no Stage 2 destination that can take money.
- **It cannot rank in Google while there is no domain you own.** Pinterest is doing the search work in the meantime, on rented ground.
- **It cannot substitute for reviews.** For picture books, the first 20–50 honest reviews move more units than 200 pins. For the logbook, the review ask in Week 3 is the most commercially important line in this document.
- **It cannot verify its own keyword assumptions** — neither stage's — without real query data and a research pass. The Stage 2 set is a hypothesis from channel research; the Stage 1 set is a starter hypothesis with **no research pass behind it yet** (§3.3).
- **It cannot make one account speak to two audiences equally well.** §5 says where that costs us and what I'm doing about it.
